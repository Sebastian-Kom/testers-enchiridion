# The Tester's Handbook V103 — Source Check and Copyedit Record

22 September 2026

This record accompanies `testers-enchiridion-V103.md`. It is separate from the book and does not add chapter commentary. V103 was prepared from the author's reviewed `testers-enchiridion-V102.md`; V102 has been preserved.

## Edition used

Epictetus. *All the Works of Epictetus, Which Are Now Extant*. Translated by Elizabeth Carter. Dublin: printed for Hulton Bradley, 1759. *The Enchiridion*, pp. 387–412. The passage for §29 comes from *Discourses* III.15, p. 236, following the cross-reference printed under §XXIX on p. 399.

The [scanned volume](https://archive.org/details/allworksofepicte00epic) and its [PDF](https://archive.org/download/allworksofepicte00epic/allworksofepicte00epic.pdf) are the controlling sources. The [Wikisource transcription](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/All_the_Works_of_Epictetus,_Which_Are_Now_Extant/The_Encheiridion) was used to locate passages; wording and punctuation were checked against the printed page images. Links in the table below identify PDF page numbers; the table itself gives the book's printed page numbers. Sources accessed 22 September 2026.

This is the **1759 Dublin edition**, not the first edition of 1758. The earlier working text contained later modernizations of Carter. V103 takes the retained wording from the historical edition above instead of attributing those modernizations to Carter unchanged.

## Transcription policy

Historical wording, spelling, capitalization, and punctuation are retained in the source passages, including forms such as “hath,” “doth,” “Honour,” and “Half-penny.” The long s is rendered as an ordinary s. Typographic small capitals, italics, page breaks, line wrapping, line-end word divisions, and footnote callouts are not reproduced. Carter's substantive square-bracketed text is retained. Ordinary quotation marks represent the printed quotation marks; outer quotation marks belonging to a longer surrounding passage are not added around an extracted sentence.

The modern prose uses American spelling and consistent heading capitalization. Its punctuation follows modern usage. The differing appearance of the historical passages is intentional.

## Exceptions to an unchanged complete sentence

- **§7:** Retains the final clause of Carter's longer sentence. Its initial “but” becomes “But.” This preserves the captain's call without importing the preceding wife-and-child analogy.
- **§18:** Preserves the author's second-person adaptation: “my” becomes “your”; “which-ever” becomes “whichever”; capitalization is regularized. The line is an adaptation, not an unchanged quotation.
- **§23:** Preserves the author's substitution of “tester” for “Philosopher.” The remainder follows the historical sentence.
- **§40:** Preserves the existing decision to use no unchanged sentence from the original section.
- **§52:** Retains the opening formula with a colon in place of its final full stop, introducing the author's four reminders.

§2 contains two retained sentences. In total, V103 has **48 complete retained sentences across 47 chapters**, plus the four excerpted or adapted anchors listed above. These counts use the transcription policy just stated; they do not claim a facsimile reproduction of the typography.

## Historical wording behind the adapted anchors

**§7**

> Thus likewise in Life, if, instead of an Onion, or a Shell-fish, such a Thing as a Wife or a Child be granted you, there is no Objection: but if the Captain calls, run to the Ship, leave all these Things, regard none of them.

**§18**

> For which-ever of these Things happens, it is in my Power to derive Advantage from it.

**§23**

> Be contented then, in every thing, with being a Philosopher: and, if you wish to be thought so likewise by any one, appear so to yourself, and it will suffice you.

**§52**

> Upon all Occasions, we ought to have these Maxims ready at hand.

## Source corrections worth recording

- The opening of §1 reads “Of Things” in print; an extracted transcription omitted the initial O.
- In §5, the printed punctuation after the first “himself” is a semicolon. The complete historical sentence now supplies the three-stage progression. The tester-specific application remains in the following paragraph.
- In §8, the print reads “wish” in both places; an online transcription showed “with.”
- In §25, Carter's example uses “Lettuces” and a “Half-penny,” not the later modernization “fifty cents.” A short market setup now makes the image understandable without the original chapter beside it.
- In §29, the source is the passage in *Discourses* III.15 explicitly referenced by the historical *Enchiridion* edition, not a silently substituted translation.
- In §32, the print reads “shall share” and supplies the commas reproduced here. In §45, it reads “doth,” not “does.” The page images settle these details.
- In §46, the Socrates sentence already matches the historical text. The author's positive treatment of being overlooked is preserved; the rejected sheep comparison has not been restored.
- In §§2 and 50, the complete historical sentences are retained, including closing clauses absent from the earlier modernized anchors.
